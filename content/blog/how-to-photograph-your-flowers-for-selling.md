A grower with a bucket of just-cut dahlias has the easy part done. The flowers
are good. Then the photo goes up, taken quickly on a phone in a dim shed with
the strip light on, and the colours go flat and a bit grey, and the dahlias
that looked incredible five minutes ago look like something from a service
station forecourt. The flowers didn't get worse. The photo just didn't do the
job.

This isn't about becoming a photographer. It's about a handful of small,
repeatable habits that take the same five minutes as a bad photo and produce a
good one instead, because for a lot of buyers, the photo is the only part of
your flowers they see before they decide to ask.

## Light matters more than the camera

The single biggest difference between a photo that looks professional and one
that doesn't is light, not equipment. A recent phone camera is genuinely good
enough for this. What it can't do is fix bad light after the fact.

Direct sun is the enemy, not the friend. Harsh midday sun bleaches colour out
of petals, throws hard black shadows, and blows out the pale parts of a bloom
to a featureless white. An overcast day, on the other hand, is a gift: the
cloud cover acts like a giant soft light, evening everything out and showing
the true colour of the flower. If you're stuck with full sun, move into open
shade, the shadow of a building or a tree, rather than shooting in direct
light.

Indoors, a shed with a single strip light rarely helps. If you can, carry the
bucket to a doorway or a window and shoot by daylight instead. It costs you a
few steps and saves the photo.

## Keep the background out of the way

A buyer's eye goes wherever the photo lets it, and a cluttered background, a
wheelbarrow, a hosepipe, last week's seed trays, pulls attention away from the
thing you're actually selling. You don't need a studio. A plain wall, a sheet
of card, an upturned crate, or just grass and nothing else, all work, as long
as it's simple and doesn't compete with the flower.

Pick one or two backdrops you can return to each time, rather than whatever's
nearby on the day. Using the same backdrop across a season does more for how
professional your page looks than any single great shot, because it reads as
a consistent catalogue rather than a scrapbook. It also means less deciding
each time you pick up the camera, which matters when you're doing this in the
same five minutes you've got between cutting and delivering.

## Show the true colour, not the Instagram version

It's tempting to nudge the saturation up until a dahlia practically glows.
Resist it. A buyer orders based on what they see in the photo, and if the
real stem arrives a shade duller than the picture promised, that gap reads as
the flower disappointing them, when really it's the photo that lied a little.
For a wedding florist matching a colour scheme or a shop buyer planning a
window, accurate colour matters more than impressive colour.

The same goes for white balance. Phone cameras sometimes guess wrong indoors
or in mixed light and cast everything slightly yellow or blue. If a photo
looks off-colour, moving outdoors into daylight usually fixes it faster than
trying to correct it afterwards. A little brightening or straightening in
editing is fine. Heavy filters and saturation sliders are where trust starts
to leak out of a photo.

## Show scale, and show the stem, not just the bloom

A close-up of a single flower head is lovely and tells a buyer almost nothing
useful. Florists and anyone ordering by the stem need to see the whole thing:
length, how straight it grew, how many blooms or side-shoots are on it, and
roughly how big the head is relative to something recognisable, a hand, a
bucket rim, a ruler laid alongside it. A wide shot of a full bucket, label
and all, does more selling work than a flattering close-up, because it answers
the actual question a buyer has: what would I be getting ten of.

It's also worth showing condition honestly. A stem with a slightly open
centre or a petal past its best doesn't need hiding, it needs naming, so the
buyer knows what they're choosing. A photo that oversells condition costs you
more on the second order than an honest one costs you on the first.

## Make it a five-minute habit, not a production

The reason flower photos so often end up rushed and dim is that they get
treated as an extra job squeezed in after the real work of cutting. The fix
isn't a proper photoshoot, which nobody doing this alongside actual growing
has time for in July. It's a small routine you repeat every time you cut:
carry the bucket to the same spot in daylight, shoot the same two or three
angles, done. Little and often beats occasional and polished, because what
you're really building is a current, trustworthy record of what you've got,
not a portfolio.

That habit matters even more in high summer, when [what you've actually got
changes every few
days](/blog/how-to-sell-flowers-locally-without-a-website). A beautiful photo
of last week's ranunculus doesn't help a florist deciding what to order
today. The plainest photo of what's genuinely ready right now is worth more
than the best photo of something that's already finished.

It's the same instinct that makes florists trust a grower in the first place:
[being straight about what's actually available, this
week](/blog/selling-to-florists-what-they-want-from-a-local-grower), rather
than polished and vague. A current, honestly lit photo says the same thing a
reliable list does: this is real, and it's today's.

## A simple kit, if you want one

None of this needs buying anything, but a few cheap things make the five
minutes easier:

- **A plain backdrop** you keep near the cutting area, so carrying flowers to
  it becomes automatic rather than a decision each time.
- **A cheap tripod or a stack of books**, so you're not holding the phone and
  the stems at once and getting a blurrier shot than the light deserves.
- **A consistent time of day**, early morning or late afternoon, when the
  light is softer and more flattering than the flat glare of midday.

## The short version

Good flower photography for selling isn't about equipment, it's about light,
a plain background, honest colour, and a habit you can actually keep up
through the season. Shoot in soft daylight, keep the backdrop simple, resist
the saturation slider, and do it every time you cut rather than occasionally
and perfectly. The flowers are already doing the hard part.
