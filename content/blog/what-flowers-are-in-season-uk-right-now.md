Ask ten people what flowers are in season in the UK right now and you'll get ten
different answers, because the honest one changes by the fortnight. A grower in
Cornwall is cutting something different to one in Aberdeenshire this week, and
both lists will have moved on by next week regardless. There's no single, fixed
answer, only a shape to the year and a current list that only the grower
actually holds.

That shape is still worth knowing, whether you're a florist planning stock, a
couple choosing wedding flowers, or a grower working out when your own gaps
and gluts are likely to land. Here's how the British flower year actually
runs, season by season, and what that means right now.

## Why there's no single answer

British flowers are grown outdoors, or close to it, by people working with
the weather rather than around it. That's the whole point of buying local and
seasonal rather than imported: no heated glasshouse thousands of miles away
smoothing out the year into a flat, identical supply of roses in every month.
It also means the calendar bends. A cold, wet spring can push tulips back by
weeks. A mild, gentle autumn can keep dahlias flowering well past when they
usually stop. Two growers thirty miles apart can be a fortnight out of step
with each other.

So treat everything below as the general shape of the year, not a fixed
timetable, a rough map rather than a live feed. The accurate, current answer
always sits with whoever is growing the flowers, not with a guide written
months in advance, however carefully it tries to generalise across the
length and breadth of the country.

## Spring: March to May

Spring opens the season on bulbs and hardy stems: tulips, narcissi (daffodils
among them), and ranunculus, often alongside anemones and the first blossom
branches of the year. It starts slowly, snowdrops and the earliest narcissi
first, then widens out through April and May as the range of colour and
variety builds towards summer.

Spring flowers tend to be shorter-lived in the vase than summer ones, but
they're some of the most anticipated of the year precisely because the gap
since the last cut flowers is so long. A grower's first tulips of the year
rarely go unnoticed.

## Summer: June to August

This is the widest, brightest stretch of the British flower year, and the one
most people picture when they think "cut flower garden". Sweet peas, dahlias,
cornflowers, sunflowers, gladioli and the rest of the classic summer cutting
patch are all in full swing. Peonies have their own short, prized window,
usually May into June, and are gone again before most of summer has properly
started. Garden roses follow a similar pattern: a strong flush early in
summer, then a second, usually smaller one later on.

By July and August, many growers have the opposite of a shortage: more
flowers than their usual buyers can take in a single week, with dahlias in
particular often flowering faster than they can be cut.

## Autumn: September to November

Autumn doesn't so much stop summer as stretch it. Dahlias, one of the
hardest-working flowers in a British cutting patch, often keep going into
October and sometimes November if there's no hard frost. Chrysanthemums pick
up as dahlias slow, alongside rudbeckia, crocosmia, hydrangea (fresh, then
increasingly dried as the season turns), and the seed heads and ornamental
grasses, calamagrostis, pennisetum and miscanthus among them, that carry a lot
of autumn arrangements once the bright colour thins out.

This is also where foliage starts doing more of the work: eucalyptus,
pittosporum and rosemary are all cut through autumn and well into winter.

## Winter: December to February

Winter is the quiet stretch, not an empty one. Some growers keep hardy
stems and evergreen foliage going through the coldest months: pine, spruce,
holly, ivy and twisted willow for structure, with forced bulbs like
paperwhites and narcissus bringing scent and colour into an otherwise green
and brown palette. Anemones and hellebores can hold on in a mild winter,
and many growers lean on flowers they dried earlier in the year, hydrangea
heads especially, to fill the gap until spring starts again.

It's the one part of the year where "what's in season" leans hardest on a
specific grower's choices rather than a general rule. Some plan for it
deliberately, others let the ground rest until spring.

## Planning around a date, not just a season

If you're sourcing for a wedding or a large event, "which season" is only
the first question. The second, more useful one is "which week, in which
year", because a flower's window can be narrow even within its season.
Peonies are the clearest example: ask for them outside their short May to
June run and no amount of notice will conjure them, because they simply
aren't growing. Sweet peas and dahlias have more give either side, but
still have a start and an end.

The practical fix is to talk to a grower early, name the date, and let them
tell you what will realistically be ready, rather than deciding on a flower
first and working backwards. Most growers will also have a frost-date
cutoff in mind for anything tender, dahlias included, so an event booked for
late October or November is worth asking about specifically rather than
assuming summer flowers will stretch that far.

## If you grow, the gaps are where the planning happens

For growers, the shape of the year above is really a map of your own gaps
and gluts before they happen. The July glut that leaves you with more
dahlias than your usual buyers can take is predictable, which means it's
plannable: succession sowing, a few reliable autumn varieties, and knowing
in advance who might take the overflow all help. The thin stretch in late
winter is predictable too, and growers who get ahead of it, with forced
bulbs, dried material saved from summer, or simply a clear "nothing much
until March" message to regular buyers, tend to have an easier season than
those who let it arrive as a surprise.

None of that changes week to week like the weather does, but it's the part
of "what's in season" that's actually within a grower's control.

## Right now: late autumn into winter

Since you're reading this in November, here's what that actually looks like
on the ground. Growers working through late autumn are typically cutting
chrysanthemums, nerines (where the weather's been kind to them), alstroemeria,
asiatic lilies, narcissus from early-forced bulbs, and dried hydrangea, with
anemones appearing where wet weather hasn't limited them. Foliage carries a
lot of the work this time of year: eucalyptus, miscanthus, pittosporum, pine,
rosemary, twisted willow and spruce are all commonly available. [British
weather at this time of year can affect supply, quantity and quality
considerably](https://floristrytradeclub.co.uk/post/british-grown-flowers-for-late-autumn-into-winter),
so always check with the grower rather than assuming a list like this one
holds exactly.

Moving into December, expect much the same list to carry on, alstroemeria,
asiatic lily, chrysanthemum, narcissus, anemone where the weather allows, with
paperwhites joining as growers bring forced bulbs on for the darkest weeks.
Fresh hydrangea tends to disappear by now, with dried stems taking over
instead. None of that is a long way from what's listed above for autumn, it's
more a thinning out than a change, which is fairly typical of how the British
flower year winds down rather than stopping abruptly.

## The only reliable way to know for certain

A seasonal guide, this one included, can only ever describe the general
shape of the year. The one piece of information that actually matters, what a
specific grower has ready this week, only exists in one place: with that
grower. That's true whether you're a florist building an order, choosing
flowers for an event months out, or simply wondering what to put in a vase
this weekend.

The practical version of that is simple. [Find a grower near
you](/blog/where-to-buy-british-grown-flowers-near-you) and ask what's
actually ready, rather than working backwards from a general seasonal
calendar. Most growers are glad to answer, and a current list beats a
guideline every time.

## The short version

British flowers run roughly bulbs and hardy stems in spring, the widest range
of colour in summer, a long, generous tail through autumn led by dahlias and
chrysanthemums, and foliage, forced bulbs and dried flowers carrying winter.
Right now, in late autumn, that means chrysanthemums, nerines, alstroemeria
