The first time a florist asks "what's your stem price?", most growers freeze. You
know what the flowers cost you in time and care. You have no idea what to say
back.

It's a fair question and it deserves a proper answer, not a guess pulled out of
the air because the silence got awkward. Pricing cut flowers for florists is a
skill like any other on the farm, and once you've worked out your number, quoting
it gets a lot easier.

## Price per stem, in bunches of ten

Florists order by the stem and think in bunches of ten. It's the convention the
whole trade already uses, so pricing this way means a florist can read your list
and place an order without translating it first. "Dahlias, priced per stem,
bunches of ten" is a line a florist can act on in five seconds. "Dahlias, some
sort of bunch, ask me the size" is not.

If you also sell at a market or from the gate, bunch pricing for the public is
fine there, that's a different customer with a different expectation. But for
florists, stick to per stem. It's what [selling locally without a website](/blog/how-to-sell-flowers-locally-without-a-website)
comes down to in the end: make it easy for the person on the other end to say
yes.

## Work out what a stem actually costs you

Before you can price anything sensibly, you need a floor: the number below which
you're paying someone to take your flowers.

The calculation is the same whatever you grow. Take the total cost of a bed for
a season, seed or plugs, compost, fleece, water, feed, and divide it by the
number of usable stems you expect from it. One UK grower's worked example, from
[the Hampshire Seed Co's guide to pricing flowers](https://www.thehampshireseedco.com/post/pricing-flowers-with-confidence),
put a bed at £232.97 to grow out and yielding around 1,000 stems, which comes to
roughly 23p a stem before anything else is added.

That 23p isn't your price. It's your raw cost. On top of it you need to add an
overhead, a share of the polytunnel, the tools, the diesel, the hours you spend
weeding and conditioning that never show up in a seed packet. The same guide
suggests farmers should be adding something like double the overhead a florist
would, because a farm's fixed costs are relentless in a way a shop's aren't. Add
that on and the same 23p stem becomes closer to 32p, and that's still the bare
minimum, with no profit in it at all.

Do this once, properly, for your main crops, and you'll never have to freeze at
the pricing question again. You'll have a number, and you'll know exactly what's
behind it.

## Add a margin, deliberately

Covering your costs isn't pricing, it's breaking even. Once you know your floor,
decide what you actually want to make on top of it, and add it on purpose rather

## Know what your price becomes downstream

It helps to understand what happens to your price once it leaves your hands.
Florists don't just pass your stem on at cost, they're building their own
business on top of it, covering their shop, their time, their wastage. [The
Hampshire Seed Co's guide to pricing flowers](https://www.thehampshireseedco.com/post/pricing-flowers-with-confidence)
describes a common florist formula of roughly two to three times the flower
cost, plus a similar multiple for sundries like ribbon and wrap, plus a design
fee on top of that for arranged work.

That's not a reason to charge the florist more, the two prices aren't the same
job. But it's useful to know you're one link in a chain where everyone,
including you, needs a proper margin to keep going. A florist who understands
their own numbers won't blink at a grower who's clearly worked out theirs.

## Where the British-grown premium comes from

Most cut flowers sold in the UK aren't grown here. As of 2024, [86 per cent of
UK cut flowers were imported](https://wickedleeks.riverford.co.uk/opinion/where-have-all-the-flowers-gone/),
flown or shipped in from large-scale growers overseas with land, labour and
heating costs a small British farm simply doesn't have. That's not a knock on
imported flowers, they have their place, but it does mean you're not really
competing with them on price. You're selling something else entirely: a stem
that was cut this morning, grown in season, with your name and your farm
attached to it.

That's worth more, and the florists who want it, the ones building a story for
their own customers around local and seasonal, already know it. Don't shave your
price down to look more like an imported bunch. You're not one.

## Keep it consistent across your buyers, and set a sensible minimum

Charge the same price to every florist you sell to. It's tempting to quietly
do a friend a better rate, or charge a first-time buyer more because you're not
sure they'll come back, but favouritism has a way of getting found out in a
small local scene, and it costs you trust for the sake of a few pence a stem.

It's also worth deciding on a minimum order before you need one. Picking and
conditioning stems takes real time regardless of how many are ordered, so a
five-stem order costs you almost as much effort as a fifty-stem one. A simple
minimum, whether that's a stem count or a spend, protects your time without you
having to negotiate it awkwardly on the day.

## Hold your price through the glut

The hardest test of your pricing isn't the first quote, it's the week in July
when everything comes into flower at once and you're drowning in stems nobody
seems to want fast enough.

Resist discounting in that moment. A price that moves depending on how
desperate you feel that week teaches florists to wait you out, and it undercuts
the value of everything you sell for the rest of the year. If you've genuinely
got more than your regular buyers can take, look for a one-off bulk deal on
volume, or a new outlet for that week, rather than quietly changing the number
everyone already trusts.

## When it's fair to raise your price

Prices aren't meant to stay still forever. Your costs go up, your growing gets
better, and your flowers get harder to source elsewhere as your reputation
builds. All of that is a fair reason to raise your price, usually once a year,
at a natural point like the start of a new season, rather than mid-order to
someone who's already relying on last week's number.

Tell your regular buyers directly and plainly when you do it, rather than
letting them find out from an updated list. "My costs have gone up and my prices
are moving with them from March" is a sentence any florist who runs their own
business will respect.

## Make it easy to quote

Once you've got your prices settled, write them down in one place, alongside
what's actually ready and what's coming. A florist deciding what to order wants
your current list, your price per stem, and your bunch size, in one glance,
not a negotiation over text.

This is where a lot of otherwise well-priced flowers get lost. A grower who's
done the work above, worked out a proper cost, added a fair margin, held the
line through the glut, can still end up underselling themselves simply because
the price only exists in their head, or scattered across old messages. Write it
down once, in a form you can point to, and the pricing work you've already done
actually pays off.

## The short version

Price per stem, in bunches of ten, so florists can order at a glance. Work out
your real cost per stem before you decide your price, then add a margin on
purpose. Don't discount just because everything's flowering at once, and don't
undersell a British-grown stem to compete with an imported one on price alone.
You're not selling the same thing.
