A florist doesn't fall in love with your flowers the way you did growing them.
They fall in love with knowing, on a Tuesday, exactly what they'll have to work
with on Friday. Get that part right and the flowers sell themselves. Get it
wrong, and even a stunning bed of dahlias won't save the relationship.

Most growers assume selling to florists is about the flowers: the varieties,
the colours, the thing nobody else has this week. It matters, but it's not
what keeps a florist coming back. What keeps them coming back is being able to
plan around you.

## Reliability beats variety

A florist is building someone else's wedding, someone else's shop window,
someone else's Saturday. If they can't trust what you tell them you'll have,
the risk of using you outweighs how good your flowers look in a photo.

That's not a knock on smaller growers. Open-field, unheated growing is
naturally less predictable than a heated greenhouse running to a schedule, and
most florists understand that about British-grown stock. What they can't work
with is a grower who's vague about it. Tell them plainly what's ready, what's
close, and what's finished. A florist who knows where they stand can plan
around a gap. One who gets a surprise on collection day can't, and won't risk
it twice.

This is also why the first order matters more than the biggest one. A florist
testing a new grower is really testing whether you'll be straight with them.
Undersell yourself a little on that first list, promise less than you think
you can deliver, and let the flowers turn up exactly as described. It's a
slower way to impress someone than sending your best photos, but it's the one
that survives contact with a real season.

## Give them a current list, not a conversation

Florists are busy in exactly the way you are: mid-season, mid-order, on their
feet. The grower who makes them work hardest to find out what's available is
the grower they stop asking.

The florists who source locally consistently say the same thing: they need to
be able to trust that a grower is currently active and that the list they're
looking at is real, not three weeks stale. One [UK grower's account of trying
to source local flowers](https://www.thedahliawood.co.uk/blog/why-is-it-so-hard-to-find-locally-grown-flowers)
found the biggest barrier wasn't a lack of growers, it was growers whose
online presence had gone quiet: an old Instagram post, a website that hadn't
been touched since spring, no way to tell if they were even still selling.
Being easy to check is half the job.

Keep one list, in one place, and update it as you cut. That's the whole
mechanism behind [selling locally without a website](/blog/how-to-sell-flowers-locally-without-a-website),
and it matters even more with florists than with the public, because florists
are ordering against a deadline, not browsing for a treat.

It also means resisting the urge to keep the full picture in your head and
answer questions as they come in. That works for the first florist who asks.
It falls over on the fifth one, on a week when you're also cutting, delivering,
and trying to eat lunch. A list you can point someone to protects your time as
much as it helps theirs.

## Price it so they can act on it, not negotiate it

A florist deciding whether to order from you shouldn't have to ask what
something costs. Price per stem, in a consistent bunch size, so they can read
your list and place an order without a back-and-forth. If you haven't settled
your numbers yet, [how to price cut flowers for florists](/blog/how-to-price-cut-flowers-for-florists)
walks through working out a figure you can hold.

Holding that price matters as much as setting it. A florist who orders from
you twice at two different prices for the same stem starts wondering what
else might move. Consistency is what lets them quote their own customers with
confidence, and it's a big part of what makes you look like a proper supplier
rather than a hobby they're taking a chance on.

## Respect their timeline, not just their order

Florists aren't buying for themselves. They're buying for a wedding on a fixed
Saturday, a shop window that needs filling by Friday morning, a client who
chose a colour scheme months ago and won't thank anyone for a last-minute
substitution. Your growing calendar and their delivery deadline are two
different clocks, and it's on you to know the gap between them.

For event work especially, find out how far ahead a florist needs to commit.
Wedding florists are often locking in varieties and colours weeks or months
out, long before you know exactly what a particular bed will yield. Be honest
about what you can promise that far ahead (a colour palette, a rough quantity)
versus what you can only confirm closer to the day (exact stem counts, peak
condition). A florist who knows which parts of your quote are firm and which
are provisional can plan around the uncertainty instead of being blindsided
by it.

The same goes for collection and delivery. If you only cut on certain days, or
collection has to happen before a certain hour to keep flowers in good
condition, say so plainly and early, not as an apology when someone turns up
at the wrong time. A clear rhythm, even a restrictive one, is easier for a
florist to build around than a flexible one they can't predict.

## Tell them the bad news early

The most valuable thing you can send a florist isn't a photo of your best
stems. It's a heads-up that something's about to run out, sent with enough
notice that they can still do something about it.

[Flowers from the Farm](https://www.flowersfromthefarm.co.uk/about-us/), the
UK trade association that now represents over 1,000 independent growers, built
its reputation on exactly this kind of word of mouth: growers being straight
with buyers about what was actually available. A grower who says "I'm down to
the last of the sweet peas, let me know by Thursday if you want any" is doing
the florist a favour. One who stays quiet and lets them find out on collection
day is teaching them not to rely on you next time.

## Make reordering effortless

Once a florist trusts you, the thing that keeps them ordering is how little
friction there is in doing it again. If getting your current list means a
text, a wait, and a guess at what's actually ready, you're competing with a
wholesaler's app that shows stock in real time.

You don't need an app. You need the same current list from the first section,
sent the same way every time, so a florist can glance at it on a Tuesday
evening and know what to ask for on Wednesday morning. The less it feels like
starting a new conversation each week, the more it feels like a supplier
they can build their own business around.

It also helps to make it obvious how to actually place the order once they've
seen the list, rather than leaving them to guess whether a text, a call, or an
email is the right move. Pick one channel, tell your florists that's the one,
and stick to it. A florist deciding between two local growers with similar
stock will often pick whichever one made ordering feel like less work,
because that decision gets made every single week, not once.

## The short version

Florists buy on trust before they buy on flowers. Keep one current list, price
consistently, and tell them early when something's about to run out. Do those
three things and you stop being a grower they take a chance on and start being
one they build their week around.
