Most flowers sold in the UK have never seen a British field. They were cut days
ago, somewhere with cheaper land and warmer glasshouses, boxed, flown or
shipped, and sold on as simply "flowers", with no name attached to where they
came from. If you've ever wondered whether it's possible to buy a bunch that
was actually grown here, by someone whose name you could know, the answer is
yes. You just have to know where to look, because it isn't the first thing a
supermarket bucket or a chain florist's fridge will tell you.

This is a plain guide to finding real British-grown flowers near you, whether
you're buying for yourself, sourcing for a shop, or looking for something with
a story behind it for an event.

## Why "local" is worth looking for

[Roughly 85 to 90 per cent of the cut flowers sold in the UK are
imported](https://wickedleeks.riverford.co.uk/opinion/where-have-all-the-flowers-gone/),
flown or shipped in from large-scale growers overseas. That's not a knock on
those flowers, they have their place and their own growers working hard. But
it does mean that most of what's sold as "fresh flowers" has actually
travelled a long way before it reached you, and the person who grew it is
several steps removed from the sale.

British-grown flowers are a different thing entirely. They're cut close to
where you are, often within days rather than weeks, by someone who can tell
you the variety, the weather that week, and sometimes the field it came from.
You're not just buying a nicer-smelling bunch, you're buying a shorter, more
honest supply chain with a real person at the start of it.

## What "British-grown" actually means

It's worth being specific, because the label gets used loosely. British-grown
means the flowers were planted, grown and cut in the UK, by a named farm or
grower, not simply arranged or sold by a UK shop. A florist based in Bristol
selling flowers grown in the Netherlands is a British business, not British
flowers.

The tell is usually the same: a real British-grown source can name the
grower, the farm, and roughly when it was cut. If nobody can answer those
three things, treat "seasonal" and "fresh" as marketing words rather than a
guarantee of where the flowers actually grew.

## How to find a grower near you

There are more routes into this than most people realise, and none of them
require you to already know a flower farmer.

- **A directory built for exactly this.** [Flowers from the
  Farm](https://flowersfromthefarm.co.uk/find-flowers/), the UK's trade
  association for small-scale cut flower growers, runs a postcode and town
  search that lists its nearest member growers to you. It's the single
  easiest way to find a real, named grower in your area. Founded in 2011 by
  Gill Hodgson at her Yorkshire kitchen table, [the organisation now
  represents well over 1,000 member
  growers](https://flowersfromthefarm.co.uk/about-us/10th-anniversary/)
  across the UK.
- **Farm gates and honesty boxes.** Many small growers sell straight from the
  farm, sometimes with nothing more than a table and an honesty box. If
  there's a flower farm near you, ask, most are happy to sell direct.
- **Local markets.** A farmers' market or craft market stall is one of the
  most reliable places to buy a bucket of just-cut, local stems and talk to
  the person who grew them.
- **A grower's own page or Instagram.** Many small growers post their weekly
  availability online rather than running a shop. Following a few local
  growers is often the fastest way to know what's ready and when.
- **Florists who say so.** Some florists deliberately stock British and
  seasonal flowers and will tell you, unprompted, where a particular stem
  came from. If you're not sure, ask directly, "is this British-grown?" is a
  fair question and a good florist won't mind it.
- **British Flowers Week.** Each June, the trade marks [British Flowers
  Week](https://www.flowersfromthefarm.co.uk/british-flowers-week/), a week
  of events, open farms and florist displays built entirely around
  British-grown stems. It's a good moment to discover growers and florists
  near you who take this seriously all year, not just that week.

## A rough shape of the British flower year

No calendar replaces asking a grower what's ready this week, but it helps to
know the broad shape of the year so you're not asking for the wrong thing at
the wrong time.

- **Spring** leans on bulbs and hardy stems, narcissus, tulips and hellebores
  among them, with a wider range opening up through April and May.
- **Summer**, roughly May to August, is the widest and brightest stretch,
  sweet peas, dahlias, cornflowers and the rest of the classic cutting garden
  in full swing.
- **Autumn** often keeps going into September and beyond if the weather stays
  mild, with late dahlias, seed heads and foliage carrying the season.
- **Winter** is the quietest stretch, though some growers keep hardy stems and
  foliage going through the coldest months. [Flowers from the Farm's
  seasonality
  guide](https://flowersfromthefarm.co.uk/learning-resources/uk-seasonality/)
  is a useful general reference if you want more detail before you ask.

If you're planning around a specific date, especially a wedding or event, it's
worth checking with a grower early rather than assuming a flower you love will
be ready on demand. British growers work with the weather, not around it.

## What to expect once you buy direct

Buying from a grower is a bit different to buying from a shop, in ways that
are worth knowing upfront so nothing feels like a surprise.

**It's seasonal, properly.** A local grower can't offer you peonies in
November, because British peonies don't grow in November. What they can offer
is whatever is genuinely at its best that week, which is usually a better
flower than an imported one forced into the wrong season for it.

**Availability changes week to week.** Weather, not a warehouse, decides what
a grower has ready. A current list from the grower is a far better guide than
any general seasonal calendar, including this one.

**Lead times matter for events.** If you're sourcing for a wedding or a large
event, get in touch with a grower well before the date, particularly for
anything with a narrow flowering window. Most growers are glad to plan ahead
with you, they just need the notice a supermarket order doesn't.

**You're dealing with the grower, not a call centre.** There's no account
manager in the middle. You ask a question, the person who grew the flowers
answers it. That directness is most of the point.

## What it costs, and why

British-grown flowers are sometimes a little more expensive than imported
ones, and it's worth understanding why rather than assuming you're being
overcharged. A small UK grower doesn't have the scale, the cheap land, or the
subsidised heating of the large overseas operations most imported stems come
from. [The price reflects real, local
cost](/blog/how-to-price-cut-flowers-for-florists), not markup for its own
sake. What you get in return is freshness measured in days rather than weeks,
a named source, and a flower that didn't need refrigeration for half its
life to survive the journey.

Most flowers sold in the UK are imported, so finding British-grown ones takes
