Ask a British flower grower what's in season and you'll rarely get a straight
date back. You'll get "it depends on the frost", or "ask me again in a
fortnight". That's not evasiveness, it's honesty. Unlike flowers flown in from
somewhere with reliable sun and a heated glasshouse, what a British field
actually has ready changes with the weather, the week, and sometimes the day.

Still, there's a real shape to the British growing year, and knowing it is
useful whether you're a florist planning stock, someone buying for a wedding,
or a grower checking your own patch against what everyone else is cutting.
Here's that shape, season by season.

## Spring (March to May): bulbs lead, then the gaps start closing

Spring opens slowly. The first flowers out are bulbs that have spent all
winter underground waiting for the light to come back: narcissi, tulips,
anemones, ranunculus, hyacinths and muscari, usually alongside hellebores
finishing their long run and early blossom from shrubs like blackthorn. March
and April can feel thin if you're used to a florist's fridge, because British
growers genuinely have less to offer this early, and what's there is weighted
heavily towards bulbs rather than the mixed, leafy abundance that comes later.

By May the picture opens up. Narcissi are finishing, but sweet williams
arrive, ranunculus are at their best, and the first alstroemeria and asiatic
lilies start to appear. Foliage is still catching up, young leaves on things
like eucalyptus aren't sturdy enough to cut yet, so stems often arrive
foliage-light this time of year. If you're buying for an early spring event,
build the brief around bulbs and blossom rather than assuming you can get
whatever you'd find in July.

## Early summer (late May to June): peonies, then everything else starts

This is the handover month. Peonies arrive right at the end of spring and
carry the early part of summer almost on their own, a short, intense season
that most growers and florists plan whole weeks around. Sweet peas start
coming through behind them, scented and short-lived, and the first dahlias and
early roses begin to open.

It's also when foliage finally catches up, giving growers the greenery to
back the flowers rather than sending flowers alone. If you've ever wondered
why peony season gets talked about like an event rather than just another
month, it's because it genuinely is one: a few intense weeks, then it's over
until next year.

## High summer (July and August): the widest the list gets all year

This is the glut, in the best sense. Dahlias are in full swing and keep
coming in new colours as the weeks go on, cosmos and scabious flower
continuously if you keep cutting them, sweet peas are still going if the
weather hasn't turned too hot, achillea and other hardy annuals fill out the
middle of a bunch, and roses have their second flush. For a grower, this is
the point where there's more cut than any one buyer can take. For a florist
or a buyer, it's the best month of the year to ask a British grower what they
have, because the honest answer is usually "more than you'd expect".

## Autumn (September to November): dahlias hold on, then the frost decides

Dahlias keep going well into autumn, often the backbone of a September or
October list alongside asters, cosmos, scabious and chrysanthemums starting
to come into their own under cover. It's also the best season for foliage and
hedgerow material: rosehips, hawthorn and cotoneaster berries, and the warm
tones of cotinus and physocarpus as the leaves turn. Autumn weddings lean
hard on this combination of dahlias, chrysanthemums and berried foliage for a
reason, it's a genuinely rich palette.

Then the frost arrives, and it's not gradual. One cold night can take a whole
dahlia patch overnight, and growers in frost pockets or further north often
lose their crop weeks before growers in milder, more sheltered spots. This is
the one point in the calendar where the date matters less than your specific
grower's specific field.

## Winter (December to February): the quiet season

Winter is genuinely hard for British cut flowers, and it's worth saying so
plainly rather than pretending otherwise. Hellebores are the standout, with a
flowering season that runs from November through to March depending on the
variety, and you'll find snowdrops, catkins, winter jasmine and holly from
growers who plan for it. Some growers force bulbs like paperwhites and
amaryllis under cover for December colour. Beyond that, winter stock mostly
comes from evergreen foliage: eucalyptus, pittosporum, ivy, and whatever
berries are left over from autumn.

If you're buying British in December, expect green and structural rather
than a full, colourful bunch, and expect it from a smaller pool of growers
who've specifically planned for the gap. It's the season that makes clearest
why "seasonal" and "whatever's available in a supermarket" aren't the same
promise.

## How to actually use a seasonal guide

A guide like this is useful for setting expectations, not for planning
against a fixed date. If you're a florist briefing in a wedding for next
June, knowing that peonies will likely be over and dahlias will likely be
going helps you build a realistic mood board. If you're a member of the
public wondering why a market stall has nothing but foliage in February,
knowing that's genuinely the quiet season, not the grower being lazy,
changes how you read it.

Where it stops being useful is the week before you actually need flowers.
At that point the calendar is a starting assumption and the grower's current
list is the answer. The two things aren't in competition, the calendar tells
you what to expect in general, the list tells you what's actually true this
week, and the gap between them is exactly the weather doing its job.

## Why no calendar, including this one, is the last word

Every date above is a rough shape, not a guarantee. A warm March brings
tulips forward by weeks. A late frost can undo a month of growth overnight.
Growers twenty miles apart, in different soil and a different microclimate,
can be cutting completely different things on the same day. That's not a
flaw in the system, it's what "seasonal" actually means: grown by the
weather, not by a plan drawn up in an office.

Where you are in the country matters too, not just the week. A grower in a
sheltered Cornish valley can be weeks ahead of one on a Scottish hillside, and
a garden in a frost pocket lags behind a garden a few miles away on higher,
better-drained ground. "British flowers" is one phrase covering a lot of
different climates, which is exactly why two growers can give you two
different answers to "is it dahlia season yet" in the same week.

The honest answer to "what's in season right now" is always going to be more
accurate coming from an actual grower's current list than from any general
guide, including this one.

## The short version

Spring runs on bulbs, early summer belongs to peonies, high summer in July
and August is the widest the list gets all year, autumn holds on with
dahlias and foliage until the first hard frost, and winter is quiet,
