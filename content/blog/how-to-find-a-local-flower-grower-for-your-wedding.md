A couple planning a September wedding often arrive with a Pinterest board full
of peonies. Peonies finish in June. It's not a difficult conversation, but
it's one that goes much better in January than it does six weeks before the
big day, which is roughly how most people discover that wedding flowers run
on the same calendar as everything else a British grower cuts.

If you want real, local, seasonal flowers for a wedding rather than whatever
a wholesaler can fly in to match a reference photo, here's how to actually
find the person growing them, and what to expect once you do.

## Start with what's realistic for your date, not a fixed list

Before you go looking for a grower, it helps to loosen your grip on specific
named flowers and hold onto the colours and the feeling instead. A grower
working to your date can tell you, closer to the time, exactly what will be
at its peak. A spring wedding leans on tulips, ranunculus and blossom. A high
summer date gets the widest choice of all, roses, sweet peas, dahlias,
cosmos and scabious. An autumn wedding still has dahlias and rich, late
colour right up until the first frost. [What's genuinely available changes
by the week](/blog/where-to-buy-british-grown-flowers-near-you), so a
grower's honest answer for your actual date beats any list you arrive with.

Couples who get the best results treat the brief as a mood and a palette,
not a shopping list. "Warm, blousy, peachy tones" gives a grower room to
choose what's actually thriving. "I need twelve stems of a specific peony
variety on the 14th of September" doesn't, because that flower won't exist
that week, however much you'd like it to.

## Where to actually look

A handful of places reliably turn up real growers, not just florists who buy
in stock.

- **Flowers from the Farm's directory** lists its member growers by postcode,
  and many take wedding bookings directly. It's the single best starting
  point if you don't already know a grower near you.
- **Instagram**, searched by your venue's town or county alongside terms like
  "flower farm" or "cut flowers", turns up growers who post their current
  stock and often their wedding work.
- **Your venue or existing florist** may already work with a local grower,
  even if they don't advertise it. It costs nothing to ask.
- **A page like Stems**, where growers list what they have and where they
  are, so you can see who's actually growing near your venue without
  messaging a dozen people first.
- **Local wedding fairs and county Flowers from the Farm groups**, where
  growers who don't have much online presence often show up in person.

Whichever route you use, check that you're talking to the person who
actually grew the flowers, not a florist buying them in and calling them
local. If someone can tell you the variety, the field, and roughly when it
was cut, you're in the right place.

## Understand what they actually offer

"Local flower grower" covers a wide range of services, and it's worth
knowing which one you're booking before you get too far into planning.

- **Full service grower-florists** will design and make your bouquets,
  buttonholes, table arrangements and more, using flowers they've grown
  themselves. This is the closest match to a traditional florist, with the
  flowers coming from their own ground.
- **Buckets for DIY** is common among smaller growers: a set number of
  stems, foliage and filler, cut close to your date, for you, a friend, or a
  hired florist to arrange. It costs less, and plenty of couples enjoy
  arranging their own flowers the day before as part of the wedding itself.
- **A hybrid** is common too: the grower makes the bridal party's bouquets
  and buttonholes, the trickiest pieces to get right, and supplies buckets
  for the rest, which you or a friend arrange.

None of these is the "proper" option. Pick whichever matches your budget,
your time, and how much you actually want to be arranging flowers the
morning of your wedding.

## Book early, especially for a summer date

Wedding season and the best of the British flower season are almost exactly
the same months, which is good news for choice and bad news for how far
ahead you need to plan. A small grower often takes on a limited number of
weddings each weekend through the summer, because a wedding order competes
for the same stems as everything else they're growing and selling that week.
Popular Saturdays in June, July and August get claimed early, sometimes the
year before.

As soon as your venue and date are confirmed, it's worth getting in touch
with a grower, even just to ask whether they're likely to have capacity. You
don't need your full flower list ready. You need your date in their diary
before someone else's does.

## What to ask when you get in touch

A short, direct message gets you further than a long mood board on the first
contact.

- Do you grow the flowers yourselves, or buy some in to supplement?
- What's realistically available, or close to it, for my date?
- Do you offer arranged bouquets and buttonholes, buckets for DIY, or both?
- Is there a minimum order, and do you need a site visit before the day?
- When would you need final numbers and colours confirmed by?

A grower who answers these plainly, including telling you what they can't
do, is usually the one worth booking.

## The short version

Work from colour and mood, not a fixed list of named flowers, since the
season decides what's actually possible on your date. Find a real grower
through a directory, Instagram, your venue, or a page that lists local
stock, confirm whether they arrange flowers or supply buckets for you to use,
and get your date into their diary as early as you can. The earlier you
start, the more of the season you get to choose from.
